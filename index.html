When Smoke Moves In, the Sky and the Air Don’t Always Agree

In the 2020 fire season, only about one in seven Bay Area county-days with satellite smoke overhead also had unhealthy air at the ground. However, every day with unhealthy air had smoke overhead. On smoky days, bad air was also much more common when a fire was burning in the same county: about 24% of days, compared with 7% without one.

By Siya, Rubina & Zaid · EPA PM2.5 · NOAA HMS satellite smoke · CalFire incidents · ten Bay Area counties

If you lived in the Bay Area in 2020, you probably remember Sept. 9. People from Oakland to San Jose woke up to an orange sky. Photos of it went around the world, and parents sealed their windows. Everyone was asking the same question: Is this what we’re breathing?

For the most part, the answer that day was no. Satellites saw heavy smoke over all ten Bay Area counties. Yet not one county’s monitors reached 35 µg/m³, which is EPA’s line for air that is “unhealthy for sensitive groups.” The Bay-wide median was only about 28. This is because the smoke was sitting on top of the cool marine layer, which kept it off the ground. The air district’s spokesman said at the time that low-lying areas were basically insulated from it (Mountain View Voice). The air only turned bad once the smoke mixed down. The median hit 87 on Sept. 10 and 130 on Sept. 11.

This gap between what the sky shows and what reaches our lungs is what this article is about. We put three data sources on one calendar: EPA air monitors, NOAA satellite smoke and CalFire fire records. With them, we looked at how often the sky and the ground agreed in 2020, where and when they agreed, and what changed when a fire was burning in the same county. Our main takeaway is simple: smoke overhead is a warning sign, not a verdict.

We start by looking at the season as a whole. Then we follow each county’s running total of pollution. After that, we look at when the sky and the ground actually agreed. Finally, we compare 2020 with other years and check what a local fire added.

The Season Was Longer Than the Photos

When we put pollution, satellite smoke and named CalFire fires on one timeline, 2020 stops looking like one viral afternoon. The worst days at ground level came in two clusters, one in late August and one in mid-September. Smoke overhead lasted much longer than that. Find Sept. 9 on the chart. The smoke strip is at its darkest, but the pollution line barely moves. Two days later, under the same dark strip, the line hits its peak of about 130 µg/m³. The photos showed the smoke up high. The monitors showed it once it reached the air we breathe. Over the Aug. 1 to Nov. 15 season, satellites saw smoke over at least one county on about 80 days. However, the Bay-wide median crossed 35 on only 10 of them. Our memories boil a season down to a few images, but the calendar shows how long it actually lasted.

Ten Counties, Ten Different Smoke Seasons

Big spikes make the news, but exposure also adds up over time. EPA says the health effects of smoke depend on how much of it you breathe and for how long (EPA). A 2025 study led by Harvard researchers also linked three months of wildfire smoke exposure to a higher risk of hospital visits for most heart and lung diseases (Harvard Chan School).

Santa Cruz is the clearest example of a county having its own season. The CZU Lightning Complex started inside the county on Aug. 16. By Aug. 21, its monitors averaged about 220 µg/m³, which is far above the Bay-wide peak of 130 in September. Santa Cruz took the lead on Aug. 20 and kept it for the rest of the year. Other counties caught a later wave and never caught up. As a result, “Bay Area smoke” was one regional event with ten different stories inside it. This is why the next chart looks at where and when the satellites and the monitors actually agreed.

When the Sky and the Street Agreed

This is the main gap, and Sept. 9 was the clearest example of it. There was heavy smoke over every county and high PM2.5 in none. Across 2020, about 85% of county-days with smoke overhead did not have high PM2.5 at the monitor. However, every county-day that crossed 35 had smoke overhead.

Agreement came in short bursts, not over the whole season. It also depended on the county. About 23% of Santa Cruz’s smoke days had high PM2.5, compared with about 11% for Sonoma. By Nov. 15, there were only 117 county-days with both smoke and high PM2.5, spread across 22 calendar days. The gray smoke-only part still takes up far more of every bar than purple. Thus, smoke on a satellite map is a reason to check your local air monitor. It is not proof that the air outside is already dangerous.

2020 Was Not Only Bad. It Stayed Bad.

Was 2020 a fair test, or was it just an unusual year? We don’t have the same satellite data for other years. So this section asks a narrower question: how long did the bad air last? Press Play to compare 2017 to 2021 over the same fire-season window. Other years had spikes too. For example, 2018 looks intense for a short stretch, but then it fades. What made 2020 different was how long it lasted. It had more days with any county at or above 35, and the bad air went on for weeks instead of peaking once.

So 2020 was not just a trick of memory. It had more days with a county at or above 35 (22) than any other year from 2017 to 2021. This makes it the hardest test of the warning-sign rule. It also leads to our last question: when smoke is already overhead, does a fire in your own county change the odds of bad air?

What Changes When the Fire Is in Your County

From space, smoke from a faraway fire and smoke from a nearby fire look the same. To compare them, we only looked at smoke days. Then we split those county-days into two groups: days with an active CalFire fire in that county and days without one. We focused on Aug. 16 to Oct. 31, the weeks when local fires were actually burning, so both groups come from the same part of the season. In that window, about 24% of smoke days with a local fire had PM2.5 at or above 35. Without a local fire, it was about 7%. You can click your county to focus on it.

If you switch to Heavy smoke, both rates go up, to about 34% and 24%. As a result, the gap gets smaller. Under thick smoke, bad air becomes more likely whether or not the fire is local. However, this is more of a hint than a finding. Only 58 heavy-smoke county-days had no local fire, which is too few to compare most counties.

None of this replaces checking a local air quality forecast. However, it does change how to read the next orange afternoon. Smoke overhead is a warning sign, not a verdict. Look at the whole season, not just the photo. Check your own county, not just the regional headline. Look at whether smoke and high PM2.5 are actually showing up together that day. And check whether a fire is burning in your county while smoke is already overhead. The sky can look like the whole story, but the monitors, the satellites and the fire maps together get us closer to the truth.

About the data

Daily PM2.5 comes from EPA AirData for ten Bay Area counties. Satellite smoke comes from NOAA’s Hazard Mapping System, and fire dates come from CalFire. For each county and day, smoke is the thickest satellite smoke area that covers at least 5% of the county. The first chart’s smoke strip shows the thickest smoke over any of the ten counties. A county’s PM2.5 for a day is the average of its reporting monitors. More notes are in DATA.md.

What “high PM2.5” means in each chart. Every chart uses EPA’s 35 µg/m³ “unhealthy for sensitive groups” level, but at different scales. The first chart counts days when the Bay-wide median crossed it. The county race, the agreement bars and the local-fire comparison count county-days (one county on one day). The five-year comparison counts days when any county crossed it. This is why the same season can show 10 high days, 22 days or 117 county-days depending on the chart.

Date windows. The timeline and county race cover all of 2020. The agreement bars and five-year comparison use the Aug. 1 to Nov. 15 fire season. The local-fire comparison uses Aug. 16 to Oct. 31 by default, when local fires were burning.
